The most common objection I hear is a fair one: *we do not sell anything online,
so what would a website even do?*

If your work is a garage, a dental practice, a plumbing round, a restaurant, a
joinery workshop or a physiotherapy clinic, nothing about your business happens
on the internet. The van turns up. The chair gets filled. The work is done with
hands in a physical place.

That objection assumes a website's job is to take money. Usually it is not.

## The work a website does before anybody arrives

Almost every offline transaction has an online step in front of it now, and it
happens without you. Someone hears your name, or drives past, or gets your
number from a friend — and then, before they call, they look you up.

That gap between hearing about you and contacting you is where a website
operates. Not to sell. To remove reasons not to bother.

Are they open on Saturdays? Do they do the specific thing I need, or only the
adjacent thing? Roughly what does it cost — am I about to embarrass myself
asking? Where exactly are they, and is there parking? Have other people used
them and been fine?

Every one of those unanswered is a small reason to try the next name on the
list. Not a dramatic loss. Just a quiet one you never hear about.

## Fewer phone calls, not more

This surprises people. A common worry is that a website means more enquiries to
handle, and the business is already busy.

In practice a good one reduces the number of calls while increasing the number
of jobs, because it removes the calls that were never going to become work. The
person ringing to ask whether you do something you do not do. The person asking
your hours. The person who wants a rough price and is going to be shocked by
your actual one.

Answer those on a page and the calls you get are from people who have already
read the answers and want to go ahead. That is a materially different working
day.

## It works when you cannot

A trade business loses enquiries at predictable times: while you are under a
sink, mid-treatment, driving, or asleep. Those people do not wait. They ring the
next number.

A website does not replace you, but it holds the person still. It gives them
somewhere to look, something to read and a way to leave their details, so the
enquiry survives until you are free to answer it. For a lot of one-person
businesses that alone pays for the thing.

## It travels further than word of mouth

Word of mouth is the best marketing there is and it has one structural weakness:
it only reaches people who know somebody who knows you.

A recommendation is a name. If the name leads to nothing, the recommendation
does a fraction of the work it could have. If it leads to a page that shows the
work, explains the service and makes the next step obvious, the same
recommendation converts far more often — and it keeps converting for people
several steps removed from your existing customers.

You are not replacing word of mouth. You are giving it somewhere to land.

## The things worth having, in order

For a business that operates offline, the useful list is short and specific:

1. **What you do, in the words your customers use.** Not the industry term — the
   thing somebody would actually type.
2. **Where you are and when you are open.** Astonishingly often missing.
3. **Some indication of price.** Even a range. Silence is read as expensive.
4. **Photographs of real work.** Yours, not stock. This is usually the single
   most persuasive thing on the site.
5. **One obvious way to get in touch**, working on a phone, without a form that
   asks for eleven fields.
6. **Proof** — reviews, a couple of quotes, how long you have been trading.

Nothing on that list requires selling online, an account system, or anything you
would need to maintain.

## What it does not fix

A website will not make a struggling business busy. If the work is not good, or
the price is wrong, or nobody has heard of you at all, a website makes those
problems visible faster rather than solving them.

What it does is stop you losing the people who had already decided to consider
you. For most established offline businesses, that is a bigger number than they
think, and it is the cheapest growth available to them.
