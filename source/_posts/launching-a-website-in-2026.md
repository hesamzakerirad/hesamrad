If the last time you looked into getting a website was five or ten years ago,
your impression of what it costs and how long it takes is probably wrong in both
directions. Some of it has become dramatically easier. A couple of things have
become harder, and they are not the parts people expect.

## What genuinely got better

**Hosting stopped being a running cost worth thinking about.** A site for a small
business used to mean a server, someone to look after it, and a bill that
arrived whether anybody visited or not. Most sites of this size now run for a
few pounds a month, or nothing, on infrastructure that does not need
babysitting.

**Speed became the default rather than an upgrade.** The things that used to make
a small site slow — heavy page builders, a stack of plugins doing very little —
are avoidable now without any special effort. A well-built page opens
essentially instantly on a phone, and that matters more than it used to, because
almost everybody arrives on one.

**Security got quieter.** Encrypted connections are automatic and free. A site
that does not run a large plugin ecosystem has very little surface to attack.
The routine break-ins that plagued small business sites a decade ago mostly came
from unpatched add-ons nobody was maintaining.

**The build is faster.** For a straightforward site the work is now measured in
days rather than months. That is not a shortcut; it is that the well-trodden
parts genuinely are well-trodden.

## What got harder

**Expectations went up.** Customers have used excellent software all week. They
notice a site that feels dated, awkward on a phone, or slow — even if they could
not tell you why. The floor for "acceptable" is considerably higher than it was.

**Phones are not a secondary case any more.** Most people will only ever see your
site on a phone. Designing for a desktop and shrinking it is now backwards, and
it shows.

**There is more noise.** Every business has something online, so being present is
no longer a differentiator. Being clear is.

**More ways to end up owning nothing.** The easiest routes to getting online are
all on somebody else's land. It has never been simpler to build up years of
presence you cannot take with you.

## The bit nobody mentions

The hardest part of a small business website has never been the technology, and
in 2026 that is more true than ever.

It is deciding what to say. What you do, in the words your customers use rather
than the ones your industry uses. What you charge, or enough of a hint that
somebody can decide whether to bother. Which of the eleven things you offer
actually goes on the front page.

That work does not get easier with better tools, and it is the difference
between a site that produces enquiries and one that just exists. Anyone who
tells you a website is a weekend job is talking about the building, not the
deciding.

## What it should cost

Roughly, and honestly, for a small business:

- **Doing it yourself** on a site builder: some money each month, plus your own
  time — which is the part people forget to count. Fine if you enjoy it. A slow
  form of expensive if you do not.
- **A fixed-price build** from one person: a defined cost, about a week, and
  somebody else does the deciding with you. This is where most small businesses
  land.
- **An agency**: several times that, appropriate when there is genuine
  complexity, and considerably more process.

The thing to be suspicious of is a quote with no scope attached to it. A number
without a written list of what it covers is a number that will change.

## What "launch" actually means

A website is not really finished, which is the part that puts people off. But
the first version does not need to be complete — it needs to be true.

Get the real information up: what you do, where you are, what it costs, how to
reach you, and proof that other people have been happy. Publish it. Then change
it when something changes, which is much less often than you fear.

The businesses that get value out of a website are rarely the ones with the most
elaborate site. They are the ones whose site says something specific and is
still accurate.
