Plenty of businesses run entirely on a social profile, and for some of them it
genuinely works. Posting is easy, the audience is already there, and it costs
nothing to start.

So it is worth being precise about what you are trading away, because the
differences are structural rather than aesthetic, and they tend to become
apparent at the worst possible moment.

## You are a tenant

The clearest way to put it: on a social platform you are renting, and the
landlord can change the terms without telling you.

They decide how many of the people who chose to follow you actually see what you
post. They decide what the layout looks like this month. They decide what sits
next to your content. They can restrict the account, change what is allowed, or
close it — occasionally by mistake, occasionally without a person involved, and
usually without a phone number you can ring.

None of that is malice. It is simply what it means to build on land you do not
own. But a business that has spent four years accumulating an audience it cannot
export is one automated decision away from starting again.

## The audience is not yours

This is the part that surprises people most. Followers are not a customer list.

You cannot download them. You cannot email them. You cannot reach them without
the platform's permission, and the permission is metered — a post reaches some
fraction of the people who explicitly asked to hear from you, and the fraction
tends to fall over time, because reach is the product being sold back to you.

A website inverts that. Somebody who lands on a page and gives you their email
address has given it to *you*. They are reachable at zero marginal cost,
indefinitely, whatever any platform decides next.

## Feeds forget, pages do not

A post has a working life measured in hours. It appears, it is seen or it is
not, and it is gone. Two years of consistent posting leaves you with almost
nothing that is still doing anything today.

A page behaves in the opposite way. It sits at a fixed address, it accumulates
relevance for the searches it answers, and it keeps working every day without
being reposted. Effort spent on a feed evaporates; effort spent on a page
compounds.

That is not an argument for abandoning social media. It is an argument for
noticing that they are different investments with different shapes.

## The search problem

When somebody wants a service, most of them still search for it. They type what
they need, and possibly where.

Search engines index the open web. What is inside a social platform is largely
not the open web — it is behind a wall, structured for scrolling rather than
answering, and frequently requires an account to see. A page on your own domain
can be found by somebody who has never heard of you and is not on that platform
at all.

Word of mouth reaches people who know you. Social reaches people who follow you.
Search reaches people who need what you do and have no idea you exist. Only one
of the three is a growth channel for a business nobody has heard of yet.

## Credibility is not distributed evenly

Fairly or not, a customer weighs these differently. A profile says you exist. A
website says you are a business.

That gap widens with the size of the decision. For a coffee shop it barely
matters. For a roofer quoting four thousand pounds, or a clinic asking someone
to trust them with their health, the absence of a proper site is a real and
usually silent objection.

## What to actually do

Not "leave social media". That would be daft, and for some businesses it is
genuinely their best channel.

Use it for what it is good at — showing work in progress, being seen regularly,
being human, reaching people who already like you. Then make sure it points at
something you own, where the questions get answered and the details get
captured.

Rented ground for reach. Owned ground for everything that has to last.
