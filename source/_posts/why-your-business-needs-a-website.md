Most small businesses I speak to already have *something* online. A page on a
social platform, a listing on a maps service, maybe a profile on a directory
somebody signed them up to years ago. The question is rarely whether a business
exists on the internet. It is whether the business owns any of it.

That distinction sounds academic until the day it isn't.

## What a website is actually for

A website is not a brochure. That framing is left over from the era when a
website was a printed leaflet transcribed onto a screen, and it is the reason a
lot of owners think they do not need one.

A website is the one place online where you decide what happens. You decide
what a visitor sees first, what they can do, how they get in touch, what you
collect, and what you say about yourself. Nobody re-orders it overnight. Nobody
puts a competitor's advert beside it. Nobody decides on your behalf that
tomorrow your customers will see 8% of what you post instead of 30%.

Everywhere else you appear online, you appear at somebody else's discretion.

## Being findable is not the same as being chosen

There is a difference between someone finding you and someone deciding to use
you, and most of the internet only solves the first one.

A person who has found your business is now doing something quite specific:
deciding whether you are worth their money and their time. They want to know
what you do, roughly what it costs, whether you are open, whether other people
have been happy, and how much effort it will take to get started. That is a
short list, and almost none of it is answerable by scrolling a feed of
photographs in reverse chronological order.

A website answers all of it on one page, in the order you choose.

## The credibility gap nobody mentions

It is worth being honest about a slightly uncomfortable dynamic. When a
customer cannot find a website, some of them assume something is wrong.

Not consciously, and not always fairly. But a business that has been trading for
eleven years and has no site of its own reads, to a certain kind of customer, as
either very new or not very serious. That is not a judgement about the quality
of the work. It is a shortcut people use because they have to decide quickly and
have very little to go on.

The businesses that lose out to this are almost never the ones you would expect.
They tend to be good, established, busy, and entirely reliant on word of mouth —
which works beautifully right up until the point it needs to reach someone
outside the existing circle.

## What "having a website" does not mean

It does not mean a large project. It does not mean a redesign every two years,
a blog you feel guilty about not writing, or a monthly invoice from an agency
for changes you could have made yourself.

For a lot of businesses the honest requirement is small: what you do, who it is
for, what it costs or roughly what it costs, how to reach you, and enough proof
that a stranger believes you. Pages that load quickly on a phone, because that
is where they will be read. Something you can change yourself when a price
moves.

That is a week of work, not a quarter.

## The part that compounds

The reason to do it sooner rather than later is that a website gets better with
age in a way a social account does not. The pages you publish stay published.
The searches you start to appear for keep sending people. The address you print
on a van or a receipt keeps working. A post from three years ago is
archaeological; a page from three years ago is still doing its job this morning.

None of which is an argument against being on social platforms. It is an
argument against those being the only thing you have.
